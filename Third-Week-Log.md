#### Session at GIS Lab: 
  - My third week was spent at the GIS Lab in Macodrum Library. The class time was split into two, the first half feeling similar to a lecture as Joel Rivard began to teach us the basics of GIS. These basics consisted of: GIS' origins, GIS' main three components (Visual Representation, Table, Analysis tools.), GIS layers and hierarchy, two types of GIS layers (Vector, Raster).
- The slideshow then shifted to represent how GIS aligns and works closely with the field of digital archaeology, and the important questions raised when using the two hand in hand. A Key point from this section was the fact that GIS aids archaeologists as it helps them move from not just recording locations but being able to interpret relationships within them. An example being, if you have a series of grave deposits in one section, GIS might help you map similar deposits found 5 km away. Then relating the two, GIS as a system, promotes the drive for interpreting how these two grave deposits may be related, by providing a visual mapping layer that aids the archaeologist into potentially asking questions like: What is located near? What routes may have connected certain settlements? What locations could people see from one grave deposit and the other? Can they see each other?
-  Joel and Meaghan then covered coordinate systems, most notably geographic with values of latitude (Y coordinate) and longitude (x coordinate), and sometimes possible elevation (Z coordinate). This translated into a picture describing how, because the earth is round, when flattened, one out of four categories will be distorted, those being: Area, Shape, Distance, and direction. Google maps was used as an example here to show that the commonly used Mercator projection, emphasizes distance and direction, because its a tool that is more often used for planning travel. And so they distort shape and area, this is why Greenland appears so large.
-  Lastly we covered the topic of Spatial Analysis, which is the use of geospatial data to study the entities using their geographic or topological properties. This form of analysis aids the identification of relationships between different entities, and can help promote questions like: What routes would have been most efficient for moving good between posts? Or, Do settlement locations correspond to specific environmental conditions?
-  After downloading the QGIS software and Historical Site data given to us from Joel and Meaghan, we set out on using the software to practice three beginner concepts: Clipping, Buffing, and creating a Least Cost Path.

#### Using QGIS
- Honestly I found the process of clipping and buffing to be quite simple, especially considering Joel was walking us though it on the big screen upfront, albeit at a very quick pace.
- We began initially by uploading our data from our excel sheet into the system, it quickly dotted all the historical sites on a Mercator map throughout Canada. The first task was to clip the province of Ontario, so we could group up all the sites located in said province. Again the process for this was seemingly simple at first. Once we clipped the province with the sites, the next step was to make the center of the polygon (that had covered the province, border to border on the map) transparent, so that only the border showed. Upon looking at the sites on our bordered provincial map, it was clear that a decent sum of them were located near rivers. Thus we inputted the river systems data onto our map, this highlighted all the river systems and a few tributaries onto our map, after this we created a buffer of 500m, either side of every mapped river in Ontario. This allowed us to get a number on how many sites sit on or are within 500m of rivers in the Ontario, of which I got 77. Following this we proceeded to zoom into Ottawa to click two points (Clipped Sites), as the next step was to perform a Least Cost Path Analysis between them. I struggled heavily at this point, but I will discuss that more in my failures section. As this was our last step (all we had time for), I saved my QGIS and have uploaded it accordingly into this weeks repository.
